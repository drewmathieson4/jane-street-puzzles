Andy's Afternoon Amble

An ant who can only ever see one tile at a time is moved from a four‑sided world to an infinite one. He notices — sometimes. Here is where the number comes from, slowly.

Answer p = 11/20 The whole puzzle collapses into a random walk on a ring of six seats. Everything below is the road to that ring.
The setup

What Andy actually experiences

He is tiny and short‑sighted. He cannot see curvature, cannot see the shape of a black cell, cannot see across the world. Three things are true of him, and they are the entire puzzle:

  • WALKSHe only steps on white hexagons. Each step goes to one of the three neighbouring white hexagons, 1/3 each.
  • MARKSHe recognises exactly one tile: home, because he left pheromones on it. Every other tile is anonymous.
  • REMEMBERSHe remembers the turns he has taken. Not where he is — just the sequence of turns.

He ambles until he steps back onto home. The question is whether, by then, he has worked out that the floor is not his old world.

Step 01

Throw the black cells away

Andy never sets foot on black. So the only map that matters is: the white hexagons, and which whites touch which. Do that to both worlds.

Count the ways out. On the ball: four whites, each touching the other three. On the floor: each white touches three whites, with three black ones tucked between them. Both worlds offer three exits at every tile. That is why this is hard.

Step 02

Standing on a tile, the two worlds are identical

Andy arrives on a hexagon across some edge. That hexagon has six edges, and they alternate: white neighbour, black thing, white neighbour, black thing, white neighbour, black thing. True on the ball. True on the floor. So his three options are always the same three:

So a remembered sequence of turns like L B R R L traces one definite path on each of the two worlds. Andy's feet are on the floor. But his memory is running the same instructions on the ball, in his head.

Why "back" counts as one of the three

Turning round is a legal move — it is just another white edge. The puzzle's own example uses it: he steps out, then has a 1/3 chance of stepping straight back. That is why 1/3 of ambles are two steps long.

Step 03

There is exactly one way he can get caught

He has one fact he can check, and only one. His turn memory says “by now I should be standing on home.” He looks down.

  • PHEROMONEHe is on home. Memory and reality agree, the amble ends, he learns nothing. Business as usual.
  • BARE TILENo pheromone. His memory promised home and home isn't here. Busted.

So p is the probability that the first time his mental ball‑walk gets home, his real position on the floor is somewhere else. To compute that we need one thing: given where he really is on the floor, which of the ball's four hexagons does his memory think he is on?

Step 04

The fold — where the black triangles come from

This is the step everything turns on, so take it very slowly.

Pick one black hexagon on the kitchen floor. Spin the entire floor 180° about its centre. Every tile lands on a tile of the same colour — the pattern maps exactly onto itself. It is a symmetry of the floor.

Now do something violent: glue every tile to the tile it landed on. Opposite pairs become one tile. Watch the black hexagon and its six white neighbours.

And look what happened to the black hexagon itself. Its six sides were glued in opposite pairs, leaving three. A hexagon folded onto itself by a half-turn is a triangle. Three sides, three white neighbours around it — which is exactly what a black cell looks like in Andy's old world.

Step 05

Fold at every black hexagon and the floor becomes the ball

Don't stop at one. Do the half-turn at every black hexagon at once, and see which white tiles end up glued together. They sort themselves into exactly four families.

A half-turn is a rigid motion, so it carries turns to turns: a left is still a left after folding. The fold is compatible with everything Andy remembers. Which gives the one sentence the whole solution rests on:

The translation

Andy's mental position on the ball is just the colour of the floor tile he is really standing on.

So “his memory says he is home” means exactly “he is standing on a red tile.” Home is red. But so are infinitely many others — and those are the traps.

The amble now reads: Andy walks off home and keeps going until he first lands on a red tile. If it is home, he never suspected a thing. If it is any other red tile, he is caught.

Step 06

Rub out the red tiles and the problem falls apart

Red tiles end the question — the moment he hits one, the verdict is in. So the only thing left to understand is where he can roam before that. Rub the red tiles out and look at what remains.

That is not a quirk of this patch — it holds everywhere. Every non‑red white tile has exactly two non‑red neighbours and exactly one red neighbour. Two ways to walk, one way out: the definition of being stuck on a ring with a trapdoor under every seat.

So Andy steps off home, lands on some ring, and cannot leave that ring except by dropping onto a red tile — which ends the question. He roams at most three tiles from home, and there are only 31 tiles he can ever set foot on.

Step 07

One small equation

No geometry left. Andy sits on a ring of six seats. Each step, with probability 1/3 each, he moves one seat one way, one seat the other way, or drops through the trapdoor. He starts at seat 0. What is the chance he drops through seat 0's trapdoor?

Write ak for that chance starting from seat k. The ring is symmetric, so a1 = a5 and a2 = a4, leaving four unknowns:

3a0 = 1 + 2a1 from seat 0: drop through, and that is the win 3a1 = a0 + a2 from seat 1: dropping through here loses 3a2 = a1 + a3 3a3 = 2a2 seat 3 is directly opposite seat 0

Substituting upward: a3 = (2/3)a2, then a1 = (7/3)a2, then a0 = 6a2, and the first line collapses to 40a2 = 3.

a0 = 9/20 a1 = 7/40 a2 = 3/40 a3 = 1/20

He starts at seat 0, so he gets home with probability 9/20.

Step 08

The answer

home, suspects nothing 9/20 some other red tile, busted 11/20

Just over half of Andy's afternoons end with him working out that the world has changed. Not because he saw anything — he never sees more than one tile — but because his own bookkeeping stopped adding up.

One thing worth a second look

All of this rests on reading “discovered” as: his turn record predicts home, and there is no pheromone there. The puzzle's line about not being able to tell home's edges apart turns out to leak nothing extra — when he genuinely does get home, the fold that took the floor to the ball fixes home only by doing nothing at all, so his arrival edge matches too. Read that clause as doing more work and the number moves.

Checked three ways: an exact-rational forward series on the floor converging to 9/20; the ring decomposition verified over 1,860 tiles with no exceptions; and a simulation that rebuilds the truncated tetrahedron independently from a tetrahedron's faces and feeds it the same turn sequence — 400,000 afternoons gave 0.549.
